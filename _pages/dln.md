**Gemini:**

As a 20-year-old Art History major, my academic domain has traditionally been defined by physical archives, gallery walls, and slide-based visual analysis. For a long time, I considered my personal digital fluency—curating visual mood boards on Pinterest, editing image files in Photoshop, sharing gallery visits on Instagram, or organizing course notes on Notion—to be separate from my identity as an art historian. However, engaging in an interdisciplinary environment alongside students in Computer Science, Interactive Media, Film, Environmental Studies, and Creative Writing has completely transformed my understanding of what digital literacy means for the study of visual culture.

In my daily academic workflow, my strategies for research data management rely heavily on tools like Zotero and Trello to track archival collections, primary sources, and bibliographic references. For data manipulation and analysis, my tools are primarily visual and spatial: I utilize open-source platforms like Omeka for digital collection curation, alongside GIS (Geographic Information Systems) software and ArcGIS StoryMaps to plot provenance records, trade routes, and the global migration of artworks over time. When dealing with large image metadata sets, I use basic spreadsheet functions in Excel or Airtable to clean, structure, and categorize metadata such as medium, date, artist origin, and current museum holdings.

Despite these established workflows, sitting in a room with peers from technical disciplines reveals the immense potential—and current limitations—of my skill set. Where an Engineering or Computer Science major sees an image file as a matrix of numerical pixel data waiting to be processed, I see an artifact embedded with cultural history, iconography, and socio-political context. An Environmental Studies major might analyze spatial maps for climate patterns, whereas I view them as topographies of patron networks or colonial trade dynamics. This interdisciplinary exchange illustrates how digital humanities opens entirely new horizons for Art History. DH moves us beyond analyzing single masterpieces in isolation, enabling us to conduct "distant viewing"—analyzing thousands of digitized paintings across centuries to uncover subtle shifts in color usage, composition patterns, or subject representation that the human eye alone could never detect across a lifespan.

Collaborating with peers across these fields has provided new avenues for developing my own "computational thinking." I used to view computational thinking as an abstract mathematical skill distant from the humanities. Now, I understand it as a systematic approach to decomposition, pattern recognition, and algorithm building that translates directly into visual analysis. When working with Interactive Media or Software Engineering students, we learn to break down complex visual systems—such as formal composition or curatorial taxonomy—into logical, procedural steps. They teach me how to structure unstructured visual data, while I teach them how to question the historical biases embedded in how that data is labeled.

This cross-pollination is especially vital when approaching the world of Artificial Intelligence. Machine learning models, particularly computer vision and image-generation systems like Stable Diffusion or Midjourney, rely heavily on curated datasets of historical art. As an Art History student, learning about AI has shifted from viewing it as a novelty tool to understanding it as a site of critical inquiry. I am discovering how computer vision algorithms interpret style, form, and content, while simultaneously uncovering the deep-seated biases present in AI training sets—where Western art historical canons are systematically privileged over non-Western visual traditions.

As a 20-year-old university student majoring in Art History, I have always thought of myself as reasonably digitally literate. I use technology every day for communication, research, entertainment, and creating content, so digital tools are already deeply integrated into my life. However, this course has made me realize that being comfortable with technology is not necessarily the same as being digitally literate. Digital literacy involves understanding how information is created, organized, interpreted, shared, and manipulated, as well as thinking critically about the technologies that make these processes possible.

Overall, I would describe my current digital abilities as practical but still developing. **Perplexity:**

As a 20-year-old university student majoring in art history, I have come to understand digital literacy as more than the ability to use a laptop, search online, or create a presentation. Digital literacy involves knowing how to find, evaluate, organize, produce, and communicate information in digital environments. It also requires an awareness of how technology shapes what I see, what I study, and how I understand the world. In art history, this is especially important because so much of my learning now takes place through digital archives, online exhibitions, image databases, virtual museums, and scholarly platforms.

In my daily life, I already use several digital strategies without always identifying them as literacy skills. I create content through photographs, edited images, written posts, presentations, and short videos. Applications such as Canva, Adobe Photoshop, PowerPoint, and social media platforms help me communicate visually, which feels natural given my field. I also interact socially through group chats, discussion boards, video calls, and shared documents. These tools make it possible to exchange ideas quickly, but they also require judgment. I need to consider tone, privacy, accessibility, copyright, and the reliability of what I share. A visually attractive post may still communicate misinformation, and a confident comment may still be based on an incomplete understanding of an artwork or historical context.

Academically, my digital literacy includes searching library catalogs, JSTOR, museum databases, Google Scholar, and digital image collections. I have learned that research is not simply about finding information; it is about developing search strategies and evaluating sources. I compare authorship, publication dates, institutional affiliations, citations, and the purpose of a website. I also use Zotero to collect sources, organize bibliographic information, and generate citations. Cloud storage, such as Google Drive or OneDrive, helps me manage drafts, images, notes, and collaborative projects. However, I still need to improve my file-naming systems, metadata practices, backup routines, and understanding of image permissions. Digital organization is part of research, not an afterthought.

My experience with data manipulation and analysis is more limited. I can use spreadsheets such as Microsoft Excel or Google Sheets to sort information, create tables, calculate simple percentages, and make basic charts. I might use these skills to organize exhibition dates, artists, geographical locations, media, or recurring themes. I am beginning to see how tools such as OpenRefine, Tableau, or Python could help me examine larger collections of artworks and historical records. For example, a dataset of museum acquisitions could reveal patterns in collecting practices, institutional priorities, or the representation of artists from different regions. Yet I also understand that data is never neutral. Categories, omissions, inconsistent terminology, and institutional biases influence the patterns that appear.

The interdisciplinary nature of the classroom makes this realization more meaningful. Students in Computer Science may understand programming and algorithms, while those in Interactive Media may think about user experience, interfaces, and participation. Film and New Media students may bring knowledge of editing, sound, animation, and digital storytelling. Environmental Studies students may approach data through climate, geography, and sustainability, while Engineering students may focus on systems, modeling, and problem-solving. Literature and Creative Writing students can contribute careful attention to language, narrative, interpretation, and cultural meaning. These different skill sets challenge me to recognize that no single discipline has complete authority over digital knowledge.

Digital humanities offers exciting new horizons for art history because it combines humanistic interpretation with computational methods. Mapping artworks and artists could reveal networks of movement, influence, and exchange. Digital exhibitions could make objects accessible to audiences who cannot visit museums. Text mining might identify recurring concepts in artists’ writings, critics’ reviews, or historical newspapers. Three-dimensional scans and virtual reality could offer new ways to study sculpture, architecture, and damaged or inaccessible sites. These methods do not replace close looking or historical analysis. Instead, they can raise new questions and reveal relationships that might be difficult to notice through individual observation alone.

Collaboration can also develop my computational thinking. Working with students who know how to code or design systems would encourage me to break complex questions into smaller steps, identify patterns, test assumptions, and revise methods. I could contribute historical context and visual interpretation while learning how to structure data or create a simple digital project. In this process, computational thinking does not mean thinking like a machine. It means becoming more systematic while remaining attentive to ambiguity, ethics, and human experience.

I am also discovering that artificial intelligence requires both curiosity and skepticism. AI tools can help brainstorm, classify images, summarize information, or identify possible connections, but they can also reproduce bias, invent references, and flatten cultural context. I would like to learn how AI systems are trained, what kinds of data they depend on, and how their outputs should be evaluated. My current abilities give me a foundation in visual communication, online research, and basic digital organization, but I still need greater confidence with coding, data cleaning, visualization, and digital preservation.

Ultimately, digital literacy means recognizing myself not only as a consumer of information but also as a creator, interpreter, and participant in digital knowledge. I want to explore new ways of combining close visual analysis with mapping, databases, programming, and ethical AI. By working with classmates from different disciplines, I can expand my understanding of both technology and art history. The most important lesson is that digital tools do not determine the questions we ask; they expand the possibilities for asking better ones.
